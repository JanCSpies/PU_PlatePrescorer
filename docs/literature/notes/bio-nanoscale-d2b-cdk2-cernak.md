# Nanoscale Direct-to-Biology (D2B) Optimization of Cdk2 Inhibitors
- **Citation:** Douthwaite, Houde, Pardo, Moran, Baird, Meyer, Mahjour, Zhao, Larrow, Juang, Holliday, Han, Kelley, Dunstan, Billings, Mader, Taylor, Boezio, Sexton, **Cernak\***. *J. Med. Chem.* **2026**, 69, 9142–9162. DOI 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5c03614 🔒 (received Dec 2025, published Apr 2026)
- **Type:** application (concrete industrial D2B campaign; Cernak lab × J&J)
- **File:** literature/nanoscale-direct-to-biology-optimization-of-cdk2-inhibitors.pdf
- **TL;DR:** A 768-member Cdk2/CycE inhibitor library was made by nanoscale ultraHTE in 1,536-well plates and profiled *without purification* across four bioassays (ADP-Glo biochemical, ASMS affinity, X-ray, cell painting) — showing you can run the top tiers of a hit-to-lead cascade in one experiment, but only for wells that actually produced product.

## Problem & contribution
Modern hit-to-lead winnows huge virtual spaces down to compounds worth assaying; miniaturized synthesis is now cheap, so the bottleneck shifted to **purification**. D2B removes purification by submitting crude reaction mixtures straight to bioassay. This paper is a full, multi-assay D2B campaign: amide coupling of **128 carboxylic acids × 6 amino-pyrazole cores = 768 target compounds**, each attempted under **4 conditions** (2 coupling reagents × 2 bases) = **3,072 ultraHTE reactions** run with mosquito robotics in 1,536-well plates, using only **84 mg of amine cores total** (nanoscale). Crude mixtures went directly to ADP-Glo (biochemical), ASMS (affinity ranking via protein-titration competitive binding), X-ray crystallography (crystals soaked in crude mixtures → 5 structures), and cell painting (phenotypic). Result: potent leads (IC50 down to ~1.2 nM), G0 cell-cycle arrest consistent with Cdk2 inhibition, structural binding modes — the upper tiers of the screening cascade collapsed into a single experiment.

## D2B / plate-design workflow details
- **Synthetic success is not free.** 691/768 = **90% of targets were made in ≥1 of 4 conditions** — but that 90% required 4× redundant wells. ~10% (77 compounds) failed under *all four* conditions = dead plate real estate. They ran 4 conditions explicitly "increasing the chances that each compound would be synthesized at least once" — i.e., **they paid a 4× well tax precisely because they could not predict which single condition would work.**
- **Zero-yield wells produce nothing to assay.** Figure 4 legend: *"NT = not tested owing to 0% yield."* A failed reaction is a hole in the biology, not just the chemistry.
- **Low yield masquerades as biological inactivity (the false-negative trap).** In pooled ASMS, compounds made in **<12% conversion were generally not observed to bind** — outcompeted by higher-concentration ligands. Of 54 compounds with no observable binding, **31 had <30% conversion.** Reaction conversion correlated with ADP-Glo inhibition (R² = 0.56). So **crude yield is a confound baked into the D2B readout** — you cannot tell "reaction failed" from "molecule inactive" without a yield estimate (exactly McCorkindale's argument, DOI 10.1039/d3md00719g).
- **Failure also causes false *positives*.** Reagent/amine-only controls gave 10–40% background inhibition; unreacted amine cores (present at high relative concentration because they recur across hundreds of wells) had to be background-corrected. Byproducts and starting materials contaminate the assay signal.
- **Yield variance forces assay gymnastics.** To avoid false negatives, they binned compounds by conversion, then pooled over a Gaussian of ADP-Glo values, weighting high-conversion pools toward active compounds. **A more uniform, higher hit-rate plate would sharply simplify pooling and hit-calling.**
- **D2B is forgiving of purification-hostile compounds — a bonus.** Compound 19 (26% conversion, unstable to workup, isolable only in 42% after a modified procedure) was fully characterized (activity, affinity, X-ray) from crude — traditional library synthesis would have dropped it. So D2B tolerates *low yield* but is punished by *no yield*; the value is in avoiding true zeros and knowing the yield.
- **The scarce resource is not material — it's plate slots, injections, and assay/analysis capacity.** 3,072 reactions from 84 mg. Material is nanoscale-cheap; the cost is downstream (ASMS injections, pooling design, analysis, well count). Pre-scoring feasibility saves exactly the expensive resource.

## Relevance to our pipeline (how feasibility prediction adds value)
This paper is the concrete, quantified statement of *why our feasibility gate matters at the biology endpoint*:
1. **The 4× well tax is our headline lever.** They spent 3,072 wells to make 768 compounds because condition outcome was unpredictable. A literature-trained TACIT+PAYN model that predicts *which condition works per substrate pair* collapses that redundancy → **~4× more diverse compounds per plate, or 4× freed assay capacity**, from the identical footprint. That is a direct, paper-grounded impact number.
2. **Feasibility pre-scoring removes dead wells before they are committed.** ~10% of their compounds were unrecoverable zeros even after 4 tries; on a naive exploratory plate (not a curated optimization set) the dead fraction is far higher. Every avoided zero is a recovered assay slot.
3. **PAYN's yield signal is essential for D2B hit-calling, not just plate design.** Their <12%-conversion → no-binding observation is the exact failure McCorkindale ML-deconvolutes: a crude-yield estimate lets you separate "reaction failed" from "molecule inactive," reducing biological false negatives. Our yield ranker feeds directly into pooling design and hit interpretation.
4. **The endpoint is real drugs, fast.** This is our "so what": more successful, diverse wells → more crude mixtures that are assay-competent → more real, testable molecules per campaign → faster hit-to-lead. Our contribution is doing the plate *design* upstream from **literature data only** (no HTE required to start), which is what a group without a J&J-scale HTE core needs.

## Open questions for us
- **What counts as a "hit" for the plate objective?** This paper's data says the practical threshold is ~12% conversion (below which biology can't read it in pooled ASMS). Should our feasibility label / plate objective target **yield ≥ ~15–20%**, not just "product forms"? (JnJ HTE label=1 base rate is 39% at well level; frac yield>20 is 28% — see impact plan.)
- **Condition-selection vs substrate-selection.** They fixed 4 conditions and varied substrates; our premise fixes *general* conditions and varies the substrate matrix. Can our model also recommend *which* of a small general-condition set to run per pair (the complementary-conditions idea, Sivilotti DOI 10.1039/D4DD00365A)?
- **Assay-compatibility filters.** D2B needs reactions whose reagents/byproducts don't wreck the assay (their 10–40% reagent background). Is there a literature-derivable "assay-friendliness" signal, or is that out of scope for a 1-week demo? (Likely out of scope — note as future work.)
- **Diversity vs hit-rate trade-off.** Maximizing hit rate alone can collapse plate diversity. Their library was explicitly diversity-selected (Morgan-fingerprint t-SNE vs 233k virtual enumerations). Our objective must be *expected hits × diversity*, not raw hit count (see impact plan objective).
