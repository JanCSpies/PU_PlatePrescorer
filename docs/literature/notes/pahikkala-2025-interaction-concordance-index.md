# Pahikkala et al. 2025: the interaction concordance index

## What they did

They ask what a pair-prediction score actually measures, and answer that the usual estimators cannot tell "the model learned the pairing" from "the model learned which entity is good on average".

**The decomposition (p. 2, eq. 1; restated as Definition 9, p. 11).** A predictor on a drug `d` and a target `t` is written

    f(d, t) = f_C + f_D(d) + f_T(t) + f_{D,T}(d, t)

"where `f_C`, `f_D`, `f_T` and `f_{D,T}` are terms depending on neither drug nor target, only drug, only target and both drug and target arguments, respectively. With the same terminology as above, we say that `f_C`, `f_D`, `f_T` and `f_{D,T}` model the grand mean, drug main, target main and interaction effects, respectively." (p. 2). The same four-way split is applied to the *observed* affinities of a 2×2 design in Definition 9 (p. 11), as an orthogonal decomposition with `y_C = (y + y' + y* + y'*)/4`, `y_D = (y - y' + y* - y'*)/4`, `y_T = (y + y' - y* - y'*)/4` and `y_{D×T} = (y - y' - y* + y'*)/4`. The **direction of interaction** is "the sign of the interaction effect" (p. 1).

Which model families are additively separable — the sentence asked for (p. 3):

> "Let `f(d, t) = f_C + f_D(d) + f_T(t)` be additively separable regarding drugs and targets, indicating it is able to represent both drug and target main effects but not interaction effects. Typical examples of machine learning algorithms inferring additively separable predictors are the ones that train linear models. Additionally, generalized linear models, such as logistic regression, are additively separable on the scale corresponding to their link functions [Rönkkö et al., 2022, Spake et al., 2023]."

So yes: linear models, and generalized linear models including logistic regression (the latter on the link-function scale). Definition 10 (p. 11) gives the formal test on a 2×2 design: `f` is additively separable iff `f(d,t) + f(d',t*) = f(d',t) + f(d,t*)`, and *nonadditive* otherwise.

**The ordering consequence (p. 3, immediately after the sentence above).** Exact quote:

> "However, the absence of interaction term still implies a severe limitation. If `f(d, t) > f(d′, t)` then `f(d, t′) > f(d′, t′)` for all drugs and targets. Thus, when ordering drugs based on the predictions, the obtained ranking is the same despite the target, and there exists a 'universal' drug predicted to be the best match for all targets."

Repeated in the conclusion (p. 27): "such predictors always indicate the existence of a universal drug, the best cure for all diseases."

**The warning that cuts the other way (p. 3, same paragraph).** Exact quote:

> "Recently, Viljanen et al. [2022] experimentally demonstrated that additively separable models can, in several biomedical interaction prediction benchmarks, achieve highly competitive performance in terms of the C-index, even without capturing the interaction effect."

Restated in the conclusion with their own experiments added (p. 26): "In our experiments and in our prior work [Viljanen et al., 2022], we have shown that some learning algorithms, such as the ones inferring linear models, can achieve a high concordance index even without being able to model the interactions at all, resulting to the issues demonstrated by the above examples." The datasets: the Viljanen claim is attributed to "several biomedical interaction prediction benchmarks" and the paper does **not** name which ones in that sentence — the named datasets are the seven used *here* (Table 4, p. 21: Davis, Metz, KIBA, Merget, GPCR, Ion Channels, Enzymes). Full bibliography entry (p. 28): *Markus Viljanen, Antti Airola, and Tapio Pahikkala. Generalized vec trick for fast learning of pairwise kernel models. Machine Learning, 111(2):543–573, 2022.*

**IC-index, defined (Definitions 11 and 12, p. 12).** The unit of evaluation is a 2×2 design `z^q` (eq. 6, p. 10): two drugs `d, d'` and two targets `t, t*` with all four observed affinities `y, y*, y', y'*`, rows sharing a drug and columns sharing a target. The domain is restricted to

    R^IC = { quadruples with y + y'* > y* + y' }

i.e. to designs where the first assignment (`d→t`, `d'→t*`) has the greater *aggregate* affinity than the swapped one (p. 12). On such a design the utility is

    k_f^IC(z, z*, z', z'*) = H( f(x) - f(x*) - f(x') + f(x'*) )

with `H` the Heaviside function (0 below zero, 1/2 at zero, 1 above; eq. 2, p. 8). So what is "ranked" is not a pair against a pair but an **assignment against its reverse**: the predicted interaction contrast `f(d,t) - f(d,t*) - f(d',t) + f(d',t*)` is checked for agreement in sign with the observed contrast. IC-index (Definition 12, p. 12) is the fraction of correctly predicted directions, averaged "over all 2×2 designs that can be formed from the sample" that satisfy `R^IC`. Conclusion restatement (p. 26): "For predictors, IC-index calculates the fraction of correctly predicted interaction directions over all 2×2-designs on a given data."

The invariance result is **Proposition 1 (p. 12)**, and it **is proved** (a short algebraic proof follows it on pp. 12–13):

> "Proposition 1. Interaction concordance, and thereby also its distribution level counterpart and IC-index, is invariant to additions of constant, drug symmetric, target symmetric and additively separable functions."

The proof shows `k_f^IC = k_{f+f_{D+T}}^IC` for every additively separable `f_{D+T} = f_D + f_T + f_C`, because `f_{D+T}(d,t) + f_{D+T}(d',t*) = f_{D+T}(d,t*) + f_{D+T}(d',t)` — the contrast kills it exactly. Table 2 (p. 13) is the summary grid: the IC column is 0.5 for zero, constant, drug-symmetric, target-symmetric **and** additively separable predictors, and only nonadditive predictors can leave 0.5. "The table cell is 0.5 if it is the only possible utility value for any data" (Table 2 caption, p. 13). Also p. 26: "IC-index is shown to be invariant to the grand mean and the main effects, and hence is genuinely a function of interaction effects only." Note the direction of the claim carefully: an additively separable predictor is **exactly** 0.5, not approximately.

**The four off-training-set partitions (introduced p. 3, formalised in Definition 1, p. 7).** Training data `z` defines in-training-set (ITS) drugs — those with at least one observed affinity with any target — and ITS targets analogously; `X_z` are the ITS *pairs* and everything else is off-training-set (OTS). The OTS pairs split as (Definition 1, p. 7):

| name | definition | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| IDIT | `(D_z × T_z) \ X_z` | both entities seen in training, but **this pair** was not |
| IDOT | `D_z × (T \ T_z)` | drug seen, target never seen |
| ODIT | `(D \ D_z) × T_z` | drug never seen, target seen |
| ODOT | `(D \ D_z) × (T \ T_z)` | **both entities never seen in training** |

ODOT is the "both never seen" case. Figure 1 (p. 4) draws them as blocks of the affinity matrix; they "coincide with the four experimental settings considered by Pahikkala et al. [2015]" (p. 7). The relation to the hold-out variations: Definition 13 (p. 14) turns any inner utility `k` into an outer utility `g_A` for a learning algorithm (train on the first `n` data, evaluate the learned predictor on the rest); Definition 14 (p. 14) then restricts the test part to lie inside `X_z^IDIT`, `X_z^ODIT`, `X_z^IDOT` or `X_z^ODOT`, giving the four variations `g^IC-IDIT`, `g^IC-ODIT`, `g^IC-IDOT`, `g^IC-ODOT` (Example 5, p. 15, spells out the IDIT one and says "The IDOT, ODIT and ODOT variants are defined analogously"). In practice they are estimated by repeated hold-out / cross-validation (eq. 12–13, p. 15; the concrete protocol is 9-fold: drugs split into three groups, targets into three groups, nine folds from all combinations — Section 5.3, p. 23). Table 3 (p. 18) is the 4×5 grid of all twenty (partition × utility) learning problems.

**Permutation equivariance (Definition 18, p. 17; Proposition 2, p. 18).** A learning algorithm is *drug permutation equivariant* if "it has no information on drugs other than their distinct categorical drug identities encoded into its inductive bias a priori to training" (p. 4), formally `P[F_{π_D·z} ∈ F] = P[F_z ∈ π_D · F]` for all permutations of drug identities (Definition 18, p. 17). The result:

> "Proposition 2 (Alignment of drug and target permutation equivariant learning algorithms). ... Learning algorithm's drug and target permutation equivariance implies that it is uniformly badly aligned with the collections of learning problems shaded with red in Table 3. The implication does not hold for the other collections in the table." (p. 18)

"Uniformly badly aligned" means expected utility exactly 0.5 for **every** data distribution (Definition 17, p. 16). The red region includes IC on ODIT, IDOT and ODOT; the contributions list states it plainly (p. 5): "We show that without having access to any side information on drugs or targets, no learning algorithm has better than random level expected IC-index on ODIT, IDOT or ODOT drug-target pairs. That is, better than random level expected IC-index is possible only on IDIT drug-target pairs. Consequently, all seemingly better than random IC-index estimates on other than IDIT pairs must be either due to random chance or unobserved use of side information."

**Is it proved?** Partly. The proof (pp. 18–19) is given in full for `G^IC-ODIT`, by a symmetry argument on the predicted 2×2 values plus `H(-a) = 1 - H(a)`; for the rest the paper says "The other cases can be shown analogously" and "for all other cases in Table 3, it is easy to find counter-examples ... These kinds of examples are shown in our simulation experiments" (p. 19). So: one case proved in detail, the others asserted as analogous, and the negative half of the table demonstrated only by simulation.

**The remedy, and how hard they claim it.** "In practical applications, this equivariance is remedied via incorporation of appropriate side information on drugs and targets" (abstract, p. 1); "Useful side information is often incorporated through feature representations of drugs and targets, such as their chemical structure" (p. 27). The strength of the claim is **necessity, not sufficiency**: p. 27 says "Its practical consequence is the necessity of having side information on the objects beyond their distinct categorical identities, if the intent is to also capture interactions between new objects with no known affinity values in the training data." There is no theorem that side information suffices. Example 1 (p. 5, matrix factorization) only shows the mechanism: with `f(d,t) = ⟨v_d, v_t⟩ + b_d + b_t + b`, the inner product can capture interaction in IDIT; for a new drug the implementation either sets `v_d = 0` (leaving `b_t + b`, a target-main-effect-only predictor) or randomises it ("Although the latter approach has a chance for random success, the expected interaction prediction performance remains at a random level"); if both are new, "only the global constant `b` remains". Side information is what lets embeddings be inferred for unseen entities at all — whether they are *good* embeddings is an empirical question they leave to the experiments.

## What the data is

Two bodies of experiment.

**1. Simulation (Section 5.1, pp. 19–20; Figure 3, p. 20; Figure 4, p. 22).** An imbalanced XOR design: 200 "drugs" × 200 "targets", label `y = 2·((i_d > 20) ⊕ (i_t ≤ 40)) - 1`, 5 % label noise, 25 % of cells revealed; 100 000 repetitions (the Figure 4 caption says 10^6 repetitions — the two statements disagree, flagged below). Six deliberately simple permutation-equivariant algorithms: global sum (GS, constant), drugwise sum (DS, target symmetric), targetwise sum (TS, drug symmetric), their sum (SS, additively separable), their product (PS, nonadditive), plus second-order polynomial regression (PR) on one-hot indices, which is randomized.

**2. Seven real drug-target datasets (Table 4, p. 21).**

| dataset | n_D | n_T | n | density % | affinity type |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Davis | 68 | 442 | 30 056 | 100 | continuous |
| Metz | 1 421 | 156 | 93 356 | 42 | continuous |
| KIBA | 2 111 | 229 | 118 254 | 24 | continuous |
| Merget | 2 967 | 226 | 167 995 | 25 | continuous |
| GPCR | 223 | 95 | 21 185 | 100 | binary |
| Ion Channels | 210 | 204 | 42 840 | 100 | binary |
| Enzymes | 445 | 664 | 295 480 | 100 | binary |

Each dataset is three matrices: a drug–drug similarity feature matrix, a target–target similarity feature matrix, and the affinity matrix (p. 21). Drug features are fingerprint/structure similarities (2-D Tanimoto for Davis and Metz; shortest-path 1024-bit fingerprints for Merget; SIMCOMP for GPCR/Ion Channels/Enzymes); target features are normalized Smith-Waterman scores or ATP-pocket sub-sequences (p. 21).

**Eleven algorithms (Section 5.4, pp. 23–24):** pairwise linear-kernel ridge regression LR(linear) and LR(Gaussian); pairwise Kronecker-kernel ridge regression KR(linear) and KR(Gaussian); polynomial regression PR; k-nearest neighbours kNN (drug and target features *concatenated* into one vector, p. 24); random forest RF; XGBoost XGB; a plain feedforward network FFNN; DeepDTA (DDTA) and GraphDTA (GDTA), the latter two only on Davis and KIBA because they need SMILES and protein sequences (p. 24). Crucially stated up front (p. 23): "ridge regression with linear pairwise kernel (i.e. both LR(linear) and LR(Gaussian)) cannot model interaction effects, because the inferred predictors necessarily consists only of the drug main, target main and constant terms in the decomposition (1), even if the domain kernel is Gaussian."

**What the experiments showed (Section 5.5, pp. 25–26; Figures 5–11 = Davis, Metz, KIBA, Merget, GPCR, Ion Channels, Enzymes respectively).** The central empirical fact, and the one most useful to us (p. 25):

> "LR (Gaussian) and LR (Linear) have always 0.5 IC-index, as they are incapable of modeling the interaction effect."

and then, in the same paragraph on C-index (p. 25):

> "However, for the other data sets LR (Gaussian) and LR (Linear) tend to have only slightly lower performances than for the other methods. As an example, for Davis, Merget and Metz data sets, the LR methods outperform kNN on IDIT data in terms of C-index, even though the latter can capture the interaction effect and has higher than random IC-index."

That is the case asked for: **LR sits at exactly 0.5 on IC-index on every dataset, yet beats kNN on C-index on Davis, Merget and Metz (IDIT) — while kNN is the one that actually has above-random IC-index.** A model can win the leaderboard measure while provably having learned no pairing at all. The counter-case is also reported: on Ion Channels and Enzymes (Figures 10 and 11) "the methods inability to model the interaction effect also leads to much lower C-index compared to the other methods" (p. 25) — so C-index sometimes *does* expose it, unpredictably.

Other results: no method wins everywhere; the plain FFNN is "highly competitive", often top (especially Davis, Figure 5) and beats DeepDTA and GraphDTA except for new-target prediction on KIBA where GraphDTA wins; RF is best on Merget (Figure 8); KR(Gaussian) is often near the top (e.g. Figure 10) and typically beats KR(linear); PR and XGB are competitive; kNN is weaker (p. 25). Trends across all datasets (p. 25): performance is highest on IDIT and lowest on ODOT; usually higher generalizing to new targets than to new drugs; higher for the binary datasets than the continuous ones, especially on IDIT. In the simulation (p. 20): "Better than random average IC-index values are obtained only with PS and PR, because they are the only learning algorithms able to infer nonadditive predictors. Moreover, this takes place only on IDIT data, because no side information beyond the categorical drug and target identities is available." The deterministic methods' IC-index was *exactly* 0.5 wherever Proposition 2 predicts it; randomized PR averaged ≈0.5 but its 95 % credible interval "may stretch even up to 0.6 due to PRs randomized nature" (p. 20), which they call "the well-known risk of getting falsely promising results with randomized learning algorithms on too small test data" (p. 21).

**Code (footnotes 1 and 2, p. 5; repeated p. 19).** Implementation: <https://github.com/TurkuML/Interaction-Concordance-Index> ("We offer an open-source implementation of a binary search-tree-based algorithm for efficient computation of IC-index", p. 5). Experiments: <https://github.com/TurkuML/IC-index-experiments> ("The codes and links to all data used in the experiments are available at", p. 19). The paper says "open-source" but **states no specific licence** for either repository. Neither repository was cloned or run.

## What carries over to this project

- **The formal reason our concatenated-feature linear model cannot read the pairing.** Our ADR 020 measurement is exactly their Definition 10 case: a linear model on the two sides' features concatenated is additively separable, so it can only ever express `f_C + f_acid(acid) + f_amine(amine)`. The paper names this family explicitly (p. 3, quoted above), including logistic regression. Whatever that model scores, it has provably learned zero about which acid goes with which amine.
- **The plate consequence, stated better than we had it.** Their p. 3 sentence — `f(d,t) > f(d',t)` implies `f(d,t') > f(d',t')`, so "there exists a 'universal' drug predicted to be the best match for all targets" — is directly the statement that an additive pre-scorer produces a plate with **no matrix structure**: it would rank the acids once, identically for every amine, and a plate picked from it is just "the top-k acids × the top-k amines". That is a crisp, citable argument for why a feasibility pre-scorer for an HTE matrix must be nonadditive, and it is worth quoting in the paper.
- **A label-free-ish model-selection criterion we may be able to borrow.** IC-index is invariant to the grand mean and both main effects by Proposition 1 (p. 12) and is *exactly* 0.5 for any additively separable predictor. That is exactly the property we want from a criterion that must transfer to a reaction class with no HTE dataset: it refuses to reward a model for learning "acyl chlorides react more often". Whether we can actually compute it is a separate question — see below.
- **The warning about a good AUC, which is the one to take seriously.** p. 25: LR sits at exactly 0.5 IC-index on all seven datasets yet beats kNN on C-index on three of them. So a high ROC-AUC on our P-versus-U task is **not** evidence that the pairing was learned, and a model that beats a baseline on AUC may be strictly worse at the thing the plate depends on. This cuts against reading any AUC number as progress, including a good one.
- **ODOT is our prospective-plate setting, and the news there is bad.** Proposition 2 (p. 18) plus the contributions list (p. 5): with no side information, expected IC-index is exactly 0.5 on ODIT, IDOT and ODOT — only IDIT can exceed chance. Our target use is a new reaction class with substrates not in training, i.e. ODOT or at best IDOT/ODIT. The only thing that can rescue interaction prediction there is side information, and in our case that side information is exactly the molecular features. This is a direct argument for why the feature block matters and why a model that works only off substrate identity (any kind of collaborative-filtering or frequency-based scorer) cannot transfer. It also means that if we ever see above-chance interaction performance on unseen substrates *without* features, the paper says it is chance or hidden leakage (p. 5).

**On the reading of the 0.478 put to me in the brief** (which came from the working session, not from Luka). The framing is *mostly* supported but it over-reaches in one specific place, and I think the over-reach matters.

Supported: our unlabeled set is built with the per-side marginals matched to the positives on purpose (ADR 013, ADR 015), so neither side alone carries class information; and a concatenated-feature linear model is additively separable (p. 3). Those two together do mean our P-versus-U AUC has had most of the "which substrate is popular" signal designed out of it, so it is closer in spirit to an interaction-only measure than a typical drug-target AUC is. That part is a fair reading of their decomposition.

Not supported, and this is the over-reach: **the paper nowhere states that AUC/C-index becomes an interaction-only measure when the marginals are matched.** Their exact-0.5 guarantee (Proposition 1, Table 2 p. 13) is a property of the *IC utility*, not of AUC on main-effect-free data. Table 2's C column is explicitly *not* 0.5 for additively separable predictors. And there is a concrete hole in the argument: matched per-side marginals make the *marginal distribution* of any one-sided term equal in P and U, but a weighted sum `w_acid·φ(acid) + w_amine·φ(amine)` is a function of the **joint**. If the published pairings are correlated across the two sides (big acid tends to be published with a small amine, say) while the drawn pairs are near-independent, the sum has the same marginals but a different distribution — different variance, different tails — and its AUC can sit away from 0.5 in either direction. So the additive model is not pinned to 0.5 on our task the way it is pinned to 0.5 on IC-index. Our 0.478 is *consistent with* chance and consistent with the paper's picture, but the paper does not predict it; stating "their theory predicts 0.5" would be wrong if written into an ADR or the paper. The honest formulation is: their theory predicts exactly 0.5 for an additive model under IC-index, and separately explains why an additive model has nothing left to read once we have removed the main effects by construction — which makes a near-chance AUC the expected outcome rather than a derived one.

Second, smaller caution: their 0.5 statements are expectations over a data distribution (Definition 15, p. 15; Definition 17, p. 16), and their own simulation shows a randomized algorithm's estimate wandering to 0.6 on small test data (p. 20). A single 0.478 from one split should not be read as "below chance, so something is inverted"; it is one draw.

## What does not carry over

- **Real-valued affinity versus binary feasibility.** Their `Y` is "real or ordinal" (p. 6) and their running object is a bioactivity measurement; four of seven datasets are continuous (Table 4, p. 21). Ours is a binary feasibility call behind a 20 % yield cut-off. Their framework does admit binary `Y` — three of their datasets are binary and they define a binary classification utility (Definition 4, p. 8) — so this is a mismatch of emphasis rather than an impossibility. But the 2×2 interaction contrast `y - y' - y* + y'*` on binary labels takes only a few values, most of them ties, and the paper does not discuss what that coarseness does to IC-index's resolution or to the size of the admissible set `R^IC`. Not in the paper.
- **Scale dependence, which they warn about and we would inherit.** The passage asked for is on p. 2: "it is worth noting that the presence, or even the direction of interaction, are not necessarily preserved when the scale is switched, as is pointed out by several authors in the literature [VanderWeele and Knol, 2014, Bours, 2021, Rönkkö et al., 2022, Spake et al., 2023]." They work on the additive scale "for its simplicity and intuitive convenience" (p. 2) and return to the point in the conclusion (p. 27): "the concept of interaction is scale dependent", with odds scale "often used with binary valued quantities". For us this is not a footnote: feasibility is binary, so the natural scale is the odds scale, and an "interaction" in yield is not the same object as an "interaction" in log-yield or in P(works). Their theorems are additive-scale theorems. Which scale our interaction should be defined on is undecided here, and the paper gives no guidance beyond "pick a link function and transform first" (p. 2).
- **They have observed affinities for pairs; we mostly do not.** Their estimators average over 2×2 designs built from a sample of *observed* data (Definition 12, p. 12), and their densest datasets are 100 % complete matrices (Table 4, p. 21). Our situation is positive-unlabeled: positives are published pairs, the unlabeled set is enumerated, and a negative only exists where an HTE plate ran the well and came in under the cut-off. Remark 1 (p. 6) already flags that their IID assumption "rarely holds in practical applications, and with DTA prediction learning problems, it tends to be violated even more often" — our reporting bias is a far stronger violation than the one they mean, and nothing in the paper addresses it.
- **IC-index needs complete 2×2 designs, and we may not have them.** The admissible set `R^IC` (p. 12) requires all four cells `(d,t)`, `(d,t*)`, `(d',t)`, `(d',t*)` to carry observed outputs satisfying `y + y'* > y* + y'`. Our literature positives are a sparse, non-random scatter over the acid × amine matrix; a full 2×2 with four known labels needs two acids and two amines all four of whose combinations were reported, which is exactly what reporting bias suppresses. Whether a usable number of such quadruples exists in our data is an **open question** — I have not counted it and the paper cannot answer it. It is a data-counter job (count complete 2×2 designs over published pairs, and over any HTE plate we hold). The one place it might be easy is a real HTE plate, which *is* a dense matrix and therefore full of 2×2 designs; the hard case is the literature side. Note also their complexity result (p. 13): naive IC-index is `O(n_D² n_T²)`, their tree algorithm `O(min(n_D² n_T log n_T, n_D n_T² log n_D))` — tractable, but not free on a big matrix.
- **Permutation-equivariance framing assumes entities with no inherent features, which is not our starting point.** Their "absence of side information" is taken "in the widest possible sense" (p. 17) — the algorithm knows nothing but categorical identity. We always have molecular features, so Proposition 2's negative result does not apply to us as a prohibition. What it gives us is only the weaker, and still useful, statement that whatever interaction we capture on unseen substrates comes from the features and nothing else.
- **A discrepancy to check before citing.** Section 5.1 (p. 19) says the simulation "was repeated 100 000 times", p. 20 repeats "in each of the 100000 repetitions", but the Figure 4 caption (p. 22) says "over 10^6 repetitions". The two do not agree; do not cite a repetition count from this note without checking the published version.
- **Not in the paper at all:** anything about positive-unlabeled learning, about enumerated or virtual unlabeled sets, about reporting bias in a literature corpus, about selecting *which* pairs to run next (they motivate interaction by resource allocation — the "public health argument", p. 2 and the worked dose-allocation example on p. 26 — but there is no acquisition or plate-design method), and any treatment of chemical reaction prediction.

- **LR(linear) and LR(Gaussian) have IC-index exactly 0.5 on every dataset, yet beat kNN on C-index on IDIT data for Davis, Merget and Metz, while kNN has above-random IC-index — p. 25.** On Ion Channels and Enzymes (Figures 10, 11) their inability to model interaction *does* show up as much lower C-index.
- Simulation: only PS and PR exceed random IC-index, and only on IDIT — p. 20. Randomized PR's 95 % credible interval reaches 0.6 although its expectation is 0.5 — p. 20.
