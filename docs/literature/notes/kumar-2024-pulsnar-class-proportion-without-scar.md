# Kumar and Lambert 2024: estimating the positive fraction among the unlabeled when the positives are not labelled at random, by clustering the positives into subtypes and summing a per-cluster estimate

## What they did

Two algorithms, one for SCAR data and one for SNAR data.

**What is estimated.** `α` is defined as "the unknown fraction of positives in the unlabeled set" (§ "SCAR assumption and SNAR assumption", Eq. 1). It is **not** the class prior, and the paper says so explicitly: "in our PU algorithms, α is not the same as the class prior. The class prior represents the proportion of positives, labeled and unlabeled combined, in the dataset and can be estimated if α is known" (§ "PU data assumptions"). The relation given in Eq. 1 is `p(y=1) = (|P| + α|U|)/(|P| + |U|)`.

**PULSCAR** (for SCAR data; § "PULSCAR algorithm", Algorithm 1). Train one supervised probabilistic classifier (they use XGBoost) with five-fold CV, labelling all |P| as class 1 and all |U| as class 0. Take the class-1 probabilities, estimate the densities `D_p` and `D_u` with a **beta** kernel over [0,1]. From `D_u = α D_p + (1−α) D_n` (Eq. 2) it follows that `α D_p ≤ D_u`, so the estimator seeks the largest α keeping `D_u − α D_p` non-negative: `α_max = min(D_u_i / D_p_i)`. It is found as the point of sharpest change in `f(α) = log(|min(D_u − α D_p)| + ε)` (Eq. 3), swept over [0,1] in steps of 0.0001, taking the **first** sharp change when there are several. No iterative solver.

**PULSNAR** (for SNAR data; § "PULSNAR algorithm", Algorithm 3, Fig. 2). Divide and conquer:
1. Fit XGBoost on all P ∪ U, take the important features and their gain scores.
2. Scale those features by their gain scores and cluster **the labelled positives only** with a scikit-learn Gaussian mixture model (`max_iter = 250`, `covariance_type = "full"`), number of components chosen by sweeping `1…c` (e.g. c = 25) and applying "Knee Point Detection in BIC".
3. For each cluster `j`, run PULSCAR on (positives of cluster j) ∪ (all unlabeled), giving `α_j`.
4. `α = α_1 + α_2 + … + α_c` (Eq. 5).

So the cost is **1 + c classifier fits**, each with five-fold CV, plus the GMM sweep; and 40 repetitions with different seeds for confidence intervals (§ "Using the PULSNAR algorithm"). It is a **wrapper around any supervised probabilistic classifier** — "Given any supervised probabilistic classifier, A(x,y)" (§ "PULSCAR algorithm") — they use XGBoost, with `scale_pos_weight = |U|/|P|` for the imbalance (§ "Experimental methods"). Hyperparameters that are theirs rather than the classifier's: the histogram bin-count rule (square root, Sturges, Rice, Scott or Freedman–Diaconis), the beta-kernel bandwidth (optimised by `scipy.differential_evolution` over [0.01…0.5] against the histogram density by MSE, Algorithm 2), the α step size 0.0001, and the maximum cluster count.

**The assumption that replaces SCAR.** Verbatim, § "PU data assumptions", their assumption 2:

> "Unlabeled positive examples have support (examples with similar features) in the labeled positive set, i.e., p(x|y = 1, s = 1) = p(x|y = 1, s = 0). In the SNAR case where there are multiple subclasses of positives, we further assume that each subclass among the unlabeled set must have support in the labeled set. Other formulations of the SNAR case may be conceivable, but this assumption applies to the SNAR formulation we have considered in this manuscript. If the second assumption does not hold, our approaches may estimate an inaccurate α, often resulting in underestimates."

They add, in the same section, that "these assumptions are not novel propositions introduced by our algorithms. Assumption 1 is a basic aspect of PU learning, while Assumption 2 follows the definition of SCAR." Assumption 1 is the usual `p(s=1|x, y=0) = 0`: the labelled set is exclusively, correctly positive.

What that requires of the data, in plain terms:

- **The positives must be a mixture of sub-populations, and the SNAR-ness must live in the subclass proportions.** Their definition of the failure they handle: "The SCAR assumption can fail when labeled and unlabeled positives are from c subclasses, and the proportion of those subclasses is different in positive and unlabeled sets" (§ "SCAR assumption and SNAR assumption"). The worked example is a 30:70 subclass ratio in P against 80:20 in U.
- **Structure in the features is required**, twice over. The clustering step assumes "that these features also correlate with the factors driving selection bias within each subclass" and that after clustering "within each cluster, the selection bias is uniformly distributed […] Consequently, PU data comprising examples from one cluster and the unlabeled set approximates the SCAR assumption" (§ "Clustering rationale"). On top of that PULSCAR needs the classes to be **separable**: the discussion says it "estimates an upper bound on the fraction of positives in the unlabeled set under the assumption of SCAR and that the classes are separable" (§ "Discussion and conclusion").
- **The labelling mechanism is not modelled.** There is no propensity model and no parametric form for `p(s=1|x, y=1)`; the only requirement is that it be constant *within* each discovered cluster. They explicitly set aside the propensity-score line of work (Bekker, Robberechts & Davis 2019; Gerych et al. 2022) as not targeting α estimation (§ "Experimental methods").

**Labelled negatives.** The method uses **none**, and the paper never considers the case where some are available. Both algorithms take exactly `X = X_p ∪ X_u` and `s = s_p ∪ s_u` as input (Algorithms 1 and 3); every unlabeled row is handed to the classifier as class 0. The framing is the standard one in which "confirmed negatives are unavailable or difficult to obtain" (Abstract). There is no option, weighting or extension in the paper for a third, known-negative group. *(That a set of real negatives could still be used outside the method, as an external check on the returned α, is my inference, not theirs — the paper does not say it.)*

## What the data is

Two kinds, and both are **constructed**, which matters for us.

**Synthetic** (§ "Synthetic data"). `sklearn.make_classification`, `class_sep = 0.3` chosen deliberately to make the problems hard. SCAR sets: 2,000 positives, 6,000 unlabeled, 50 continuous features; the unlabeled set is `k%` positives whose labels were flipped to 0 plus `(100−k)%` negatives. SNAR sets: six labels, 0 negative and 1–5 positive subtypes, 2,000 positives (400 per subtype), 6,000 unlabeled; "The unlabeled positives were markedly SNAR, with the five subclasses comprising 1/31, 2/31, 4/31, 8/31, and 16/31 of the unlabeled positives". 40 datasets per fraction, seeds 0–39.

**Real ML benchmarks** (§ "Experimental methods", Table 1). SCAR: KDD Cup 2004 particle physics (50,000 rows, 77 features), CDC diabetes health indicators (253,680 / 21), Magic gamma telescope (19,020 / 10), Electrical grid stability (10,000 / 13), Wilt (4,839 / 5), Mice protein expression (1,080 / 77). SNAR: Anuran calls, dry bean, Room occupancy estimation, Smartphone, Letter recognition, Statlog (Shuttle); row and feature counts `[n]`.

The construction step is the important sentence (§ "ML benchmark datasets"): "To add k% positive examples to the unlabeled set, the labels of m randomly selected positive records were flipped from 1 to 0, where m = k|U|/(100−k). In order to satisfy the SNAR assumption, positive examples were selected from every positive subclass for label flipping while ensuring a distinct ratio of positive examples in the positive and unlabeled sets in SNAR datasets." That is, every unlabeled set in the paper was **built by the authors out of fully labelled data, with α set by them**. Nothing here was an unlabeled set observed in nature.

**Range of true α tested** (§ "Synthetic data", § "DEDPUL vs. PULSNAR: alpha estimation", Table 2 caption): 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50% for the synthetic and SNAR benchmark work, and 1% through 80% (0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80) for the diabetes SCAR timing runs. Class imbalance was varied as P 5,000 against U of 5,000 / 25,000 / 50,000 / 100,000.

**How well it works.** Qualitatively, by figure, not as a tabulated error — the paper reports mean estimated α with 95% CI against a true-α bar in Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 14 and describes closeness in words; **no numeric MAE or error table for α is given**. On synthetic SNAR data "only PULSNAR's estimates were close to the true α; other algorithms either overestimated or underestimated α for some or all fractions" (§ "Synthetic datasets"), and with five true subtypes "PULSNAR always estimated either five or six clusters". On the SNAR benchmarks "only PULSNAR provided close estimates for all fractions", while "For fractions ≥ 15%, KM1, KM2, TIcE, DEDPUL, and PULSCAR usually underestimated α across all datasets" (§ "SNAR data", Results). Against DEDPUL on the large synthetic SNAR sets, "for larger fractions (20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%), it underestimated α […] The estimated α by the PULSNAR method was close to the true α for all fractions and sample sizes" (§ "DEDPUL vs. PULSNAR: alpha estimation"). The one hard number for the estimator itself is the Fig. 1 illustration: estimated α = 10.68% against ground truth 10%. Speed, Table 2: PULSCAR 3.19 min and PULSNAR 14.07 min on P 5,000 / U 100,000, against DEDPUL 159.59 and TIcE 706.50; KM failed.

So on range: **the method is validated across 1–50% true α, not only at low α** — this is the opposite of the usual gap, and the paper makes a point of it, noting that "few PU algorithms have been assessed when α is small (≤5%)" (Introduction) and that the weakness of the competing methods appears **above** 15%, not below.

**Stated limitations and failure modes**, in their words (§ "Discussion and conclusion" unless noted):

- "A limitation of our approach is that it relies on knowing whether the data are SCAR or SNAR to effectively select between PULSCAR or PULSNAR for α estimation."
- The knee-point rule "always returns more than one cluster", and "When more than one clusters are used with PULSNAR on SCAR data, it can result in PULSNAR overestimating α as two near-identical positive types cannot be distinguished and get counted more than once. This α overestimation would adversely affect classification performance."
- The upper-bound guarantee is lost in the SNAR case: "with the PULSNAR clustering heuristic, we cannot guarantee an upper bound as we can in the SCAR case."
- PULSCAR is a bound from above, approached with bias at small α: "our estimates were all close to the actual ground truth α, albeit with greater percent overestimation with smaller α values […] Overestimation may occur due to errors in estimating the continuous density functions with finite sample sizes, and issues of lack of support between positives and unlabeled in the data generation process due to random sampling."
- Separability drives everything: "The rather poor performance of KM1, KM2, and TIcE in some datasets seems to be linked to the difficulty of the classification problems. Our simulated data used a low class separability (class_sep = 0.3), and experiments we did with higher class separability (class_sep = 0.9) resulted in better α estimation for those algorithms." High-separability results were excluded from the article.
- Breaking assumption 2 biases **downwards**: "If the second assumption does not hold, our approaches may estimate an inaccurate α, often resulting in underestimates" (§ "PU data assumptions").
- Calibration is worse for SNAR than SCAR and worse at small fractions (§ "Probability calibration").

## What carries over to this project

- **It answers our gating question in the right currency.** What we need is the fraction of positives among our 102,200 constructed pairs, which is exactly their α and explicitly **not** the class prior (§ "PU data assumptions"). If we go this route we must report α, and convert to π only through Eq. 1, `π = (|P| + α|U|)/(|P| + |U|)`.
- **It is validated where our α is suspected to be, not only at low α.** The 20–50% band is where it was tested and where the competing methods (KM1, KM2, TIcE, DEDPUL, and their own SCAR algorithm PULSCAR) were shown to fail (§ "SNAR data", § "DEDPUL vs. PULSNAR: alpha estimation"). Our plate's 29.1% and PAYN's 58–77% sit inside or just above that band, so we would not be extrapolating in α — this is the one worry in the brief that the paper does retire.
- **Their diagnosis of SNAR is ours.** Their mechanism — severe cases get diagnosed, so labelling depends on features — is structurally our ADR 019 finding that a reaction is labelled by being published and publication depends on the chemistry. The "subclass" reading is a usable frame for us: publication favours some regions of substrate space over others, and clustering the published positives before estimating is the move they propose.
- **An argument against the current spy threshold, from their results.** The SCAR-based estimators "usually underestimated α" for fractions ≥ 15% (§ "SNAR data", Results). A SCAR-flavoured device that underestimates how many positives hide in U is exactly the failure mode that would make our spy threshold declare a contaminated set reliable. That is independent support for treating ADR 013's choice as needing a measurement, not a reason to adopt this particular code.
- **The cost is affordable and the method is classifier-agnostic.** It wraps any probabilistic classifier (§ "PULSCAR algorithm"), so it would sit on whatever model we already score with; 1 + c fits with five-fold CV, and 14.07 min at P 5,000 / U 100,000 (Table 2) is the right order for our 11,396 / 102,200.
- **PULSCAR alone is cheap enough to run as a first reading** (3.19 min, Table 2) — and because on SNAR data it underestimates at high α, a PULSCAR number that already comes out high would be a lower bound worth having.

## What does not carry over

- **Our unlabeled set is not a sample from nature, and theirs was not either — so this does not by itself close the route.** This is worth being exact about, because it was the question. Nothing in their estimator reads the unlabeled set as observed: the assumptions are distributional (Eq. 2, `D_u = α D_p + (1−α) D_n`; assumption 2's support condition; separability). And every dataset they validate on was constructed by them, by flipping `m = k|U|/(100−k)` positive labels to 0 with α chosen (§ "ML benchmark datasets") — in their experiments α is a property of their sampler, just as ours is a property of our sampler. **So the method does not silently assume an observed unlabeled set, and the route is not closed on that ground.**
- **But their construction and ours differ in the one way that decides whether assumption 2 holds, and ours breaks the separability the estimator needs.** They moved *known* positives into U, so the unlabeled positives were literally relabelled copies of labelled positives and support held by construction. We invented pairs that were never observed, whose truth is unknown; their support in our positive set is not something we arranged, and assumption 2 failing gives an **underestimate** (§ "PU data assumptions"). Worse, PULSCAR's bound needs the classes separable (§ "Discussion and conclusion"), and our sampler matches the per-side marginals on purpose (ADR 013, ADR 015) in order to remove exactly that separability. If `D_u` and `D_p` nearly coincide, `α_max = min(D_u_i/D_p_i)` is driven toward 1. A near-1 α from this method on our data would therefore be uninterpretable: it is the same reading whether our U really is a third positive or whether our matching simply worked. Any run has to carry a control that distinguishes the two.
- **The clustering step assumes the gain-ranked features track the selection bias** ("this assumes that these features also correlate with the factors driving selection bias within each subclass", § "Clustering rationale"). For us the features that separate P from U are the features our sampler moved, which is not the publication bias — it is the sampler. That assumption is the one most likely to be false here, and it is an assumption, not a result.
- **Their SNAR-ness is a subclass-proportion mismatch between P and U.** Ours cannot be that in the same way: our U substrates were drawn from the published substrates themselves, within heavy-atom decile, so U's composition is tied to P's by construction rather than differing from it freely. Whether our labelling mechanism is of the form their method handles is **not** settled by this paper.
- **Our 1,121 real negatives are wasted by this method.** It has no input for them (Algorithms 1 and 3 take only `X_p ∪ X_u`), and the paper offers no extension. They are more useful to us as an independent check on any α this returns than as an input.
- **No numeric accuracy to quote.** Accuracy is reported as figures and prose, so "PULSNAR is accurate to within x%" is not a sentence the paper supports. Only the Fig. 1 illustration (10.68% against 10%) and the cluster-count result (five or six clusters for five true subtypes) are numbers.
- **The SCAR-or-SNAR decision is left to the user**, and their own knee-point rule always returns more than one cluster, which overestimates α when the data are in fact SCAR. We have ADR 019 saying SCAR fails for us, so we would use PULSNAR — but we would then have no upper-bound guarantee at all (§ "Discussion and conclusion").
- The domains are healthcare, physics, beans and bots. Nothing chemical, nothing about reaction yield, nothing about reporting bias in the literature.
